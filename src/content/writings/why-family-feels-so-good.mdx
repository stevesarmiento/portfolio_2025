## Let me in the beta, I NEED it.

I remember the day I finally got into the TestFlight. I was ecstatic, tunnel-visioned on the download indicator of the app icon on my home page, thinking about which alternate icon I was going to choose to represent Family on my phone.

I honestly don't think I had ever been this excited to download an iOS app before. The anticipation was killing all productivity that evening as I let the design dopamine rush straight into my veins; it was a euphoric moment, to say the least.

I'd been seeing their Twitter posts for months. Every post was a banger; this was hands down the most well-designed and thought-out wallet application in crypto.

I thought I knew great wallet design, but this just raised the bar.

Hell, I wasn't even an Ethereum user anymore. The chain was slow af and felt old; I was fully into the Solana community at the time, **but I still needed it**.

## Hooked from first interaction

I remember going through the onboarding and thinking, _these guys are fucking good, man_. I mean, seriously, who puts this much thought into micro-interactions, flows, etc.?

_They simply were the best._

I had created over 30 wallets just to rewatch the creation flow. Who does THAT? A design simp... That's the day I learned you **don't fuck with Family**. You just need to raise your own bar because these guys just inspired the next generation of designers and engineers.

Creating a wallet is the most basic of tasks in crypto; it's a level 1 user quest. The basic flow is: click a button, write down your seed phrase, and voilà, you're in the club. Don't show anyone these random 12–24 words OR ELSE.

This flow also usually has the least friction because crypto, in general, is pretty complicated. Most product builders add some content slider detailing what you're about to get yourself into, probably scaring half the new entrants and pissing off the power users who just want to make a quick wallet.

Family, from the start, really cares for the user through its various micro-interactions, using motion to guide them visually and bring joy to tedious tasks—from writing down a seed phrase to waiting for a wallet to be created and the app to move to the next state of "you are ready to interact with crypto things now."

A simple wallet-loading state gives shape to the work a user cannot see when they hit "Create Wallet"; it makes the final state feel earned. For a product built around trust, that reassurance matters.

## Call it progressive disclosure

A wallet can do a lot, but it should not have to announce every possibility, imo. Usually, wallets highlight the actions a user can take dead center under the value: "Send," "Receive," "Swap," and "Discover."

Family thoughtfully curates user actions across the app. The UI knows when a user needs them and hides them otherwise, until the user actually wants to use them.

This was the first time I'd seen this expandable menu system for actions in a crypto-based application. It's a common pattern in Web2—e.g., Twitter uses it for its create-a-tweet action button. It seems logical to bring it to Web3; now I'm surprised no one did it before. A lot of wallets now try to replicate this interaction, like Fuse and Umbra.

<FamilyActionMenuStudy />

Family also hides the action menu based on the view you're in; as you move between menus, it disappears and reappears alongside the other relevant actions.

## The feedback equalizer flow

Reporting a bug is rarely an area where a builder chooses to be thoughtful, if at all. It's usually, "Hey, here's my email. You can go through the process of figuring out how to email me your thoughts, and I'll get back to you if/when I decipher your ramblings."

Family taught me that you can just be better here; _you can specifically ask for what you need._

Click **Help & Support**, and you instantly have your hand held while sharing your wisdom or hate speech with the team. It's actually brilliant how simple yet thoughtful the user experience is here.

The feedback flow makes YOU choose; it forces specificity and gives you the guardrails you need to supply the right feedback because, let's face it, in product development, not all feedback is created equal.

They provide the framework to make it equal.
